## Course Overview

The course begins with an introduction to the scientific method and the fundamental principles of physics. It then explores kinematics (one and two-dimensional motion), circular motion, and Galilean transformations, followed by dynamics, covering Newton’s laws, Hooke’s law, friction, harmonic motion, pendulums, angular momentum, and non-inertial reference frames. The course introduces the concepts of work and energy, examining kinetic energy, conservative forces, potential energy, and the principles of energy conservation. It then investigates the dynamics of systems of material points, focusing on the center of mass, conservation laws, the fundamental equations of motion, collisions, and variable-mass systems. The study of rigid-body dynamics follows, addressing rigid-body kinematics, moments of inertia, rotation about a fixed axis, free rigid-body motion, collisions, and equilibrium. The course concludes the mechanics section with an introduction to fluid statics and dynamics.

The second part of the course focuses on thermodynamics, beginning with the concept of thermodynamic equilibrium and the fundamental principles governing thermal systems. The first law of thermodynamics is introduced alongside the concepts of temperature, heat, internal energy, and adiabatic systems. Thermodynamic transformations are then examined, with particular attention to heat and work, calorimetry, isothermal processes, and phase transitions. The ideal gas serves as a fundamental model for understanding thermodynamic behavior, while cyclic transformations and the kinetic theory of gases provide further insight into the microscopic interpretation of macroscopic phenomena. The course then explores the second law of thermodynamics and its principal formulations, including those of Kelvin–Planck and Clausius. The distinction between reversible and irreversible transformations is examined, followed by a study of the Carnot cycle, Carnot’s theorem, and the concept of absolute temperature. Finally, the course addresses entropy and its role in thermodynamic processes, covering the Clausius theorem, temperature–entropy (T–S) diagrams, the principle of entropy increase, and the thermodynamic interpretation of the arrow of time.


## Goals

By the end of the course, students will have acquired knowledge of the fundamental laws of mechanics and thermodynamics, as well as the models that describe them. In particular, students will have understood and will be familiar with the issues related to the mechanics of a material point, systems of material points, rigid bodies, fluids, and thermodynamics.

## Prerequisites

- Knowledge and proficiency in basic mathematics: 
  - algebra, equations and inequalities
  - trigonometry
  - logarithms and exponential functions
- Knowledge of and ability to apply differential and integral calculus

## Learning assessment

Assessment of learning for each module is conducted through written and oral exams. The written exams, which last at least two hours, consist of solving exercises based on the course modules, with a level of difficulty similar to the exercises and problems covered during the course. Upon passing the written exam, students proceed to the oral exam, which consists of a discussion of the written exam and questions on the course topics.
